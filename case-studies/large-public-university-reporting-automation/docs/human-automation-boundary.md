# Human / Automation Boundary

A central design decision was determining which work should be performed by technology and which work should remain with people.

## Technology handles

The automated layer was responsible for work that could be represented as stable, repeatable processing rules, including:

- repeatable data transformation;
- recurring preparation logic across applicable reporting deliverables;
- consistent processing of current source data; and
- repetitive manipulation that did not require contextual judgment.

## People decide

Human responsibility remained for work requiring context, interpretation, or accountability, including:

- determining whether source information was appropriate and current;
- interpreting exceptions;
- determining necessary follow-up;
- deciding how managers or administrators should respond; and
- retaining accountability for the reporting process.

## Design principle

The project was not designed around the question, "How much can be automated?"

It was designed around a more useful question:

> **Which work is sufficiently repeatable for technology to perform reliably, and where does human judgment create value?**

That distinction is the foundation of Gracelynd's Capacity Recovery approach:

**Technology handles repeatability. People handle judgment. The organization recovers capacity.**

## Why this matters

Automating deterministic work can reduce unnecessary manual effort without weakening controls. Attempting to automate judgment-heavy work merely to increase an automation percentage can create new operational risk.

The appropriate boundary therefore depends on the nature of the work, not on the availability of a particular technology.
